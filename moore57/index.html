The missing Moore graph

Where the constraints bind, and how far short of brute force that leaves us.

A Moore graph of degree 57 and diameter 2 would be a strongly regular graph with parameters (3250, 57, 0, 1): 57-regular, girth 5, 92,625 edges, 58,094,400 pentagons, spectrum 571 71729 (−8)1520. Degrees 2, 3 and 7 give the pentagon, the Petersen graph and the Hoffman–Singleton graph. Degree 57 is the only case left, open since 1960.

The question I set out to answer was practical: do the known results constrain the graph tightly enough that the remainder can be handed to a brute-force search? They do not, and the margin is not close. What follows is the audit, with everything checked in code.

The reachable frontier is 20 of the 57 branches the graph needs. The pruned search tree for the honest search is around 106067 nodes against a universe-wide budget of about 10120. The strongest structural constraint known — |Aut| ≤ 375, now of odd order — is worth 102.6.

Nothing classical rejects it

Integral spectrum, both Krein conditions, the absolute bound, a ratio bound of exactly 400, a clique bound consistent with triangle-freeness. Every standard test passes, which is why the problem survives: it has to be settled by construction, or by an argument specific to this parameter set. Each formula is validated against Petersen and Hoffman–Singleton first, including a brute-force pentagon count confirming the closed form n·C(k,2)·(k−1)/5.

The reduction

Root the graph. It has 57 neighbours, each with 56 further children: 57 blocks of 56. Girth 5 forces the edges between any two blocks to be a perfect matching σij, and is equivalent to the condition that every product around a 3-cycle or 4-cycle of blocks is a derangement. So the graph is exactly an edge-labelling of K57 by 1596 permutations of 56 points (1540 after gauge-fixing), subject to 29,260 triangle and 1,185,030 quadrilateral constraints. A structure on t < 57 blocks is a t-subgraph.

The reduction is implemented in both directions and checked end to end: Petersen and Hoffman–Singleton decompose into σ-tables, the tables satisfy the conditions, the tables rebuild the original graphs, and perturbing a single bijection breaks both.

The frontier, reproduced

Growing a labelling one branch at a time with CP-SAT reaches t = 19 of 57 and stalls there across every restart at 90 seconds per branch; the published frontier is 20. The certificate is verified twice — arithmetically, and by building the graph it describes: 1084 vertices, girth 5, with 19 vertices already at their full degree 57 and 1064 vertices at 19 of 57.

The ansatz that gets you there cannot find Hoffman–Singleton

Requiring every σij to lie in one regular cyclic group collapses the problem to arithmetic in Z56, which is what makes t = 20 reachable at all. But it is not a free assumption: if all the bijections lie in a common regular abelian group, then acting by a generator on every block is an automorphism of order k−1 fixing the root and all k of its neighbours. Searching that model exhaustively where the answer is known:

The group theory agrees, and can be read straight off the σ-table: an automorphism fixing the root and every layer-1 vertex acts on block i by some πi with πj σij = σij πi, so the whole group is computable by inspection.

At degree 7 the ansatz has nothing to find, which is why the exhaustive search stalls two branches short. At degree 57 the assumed automorphism has order 56 — even — which the no-involutions theorem forbids outright.

So t = 20 is not the frontier of the problem. It is the ceiling of a method that provably cannot reach 57, and that would have missed the last Moore graph anyone actually found.

Exact counts confirm the estimator's accuracy early on: 265 valid 3-block structures at degree 7, which the estimate gives exactly, and 101,040 valid 4-block structures against an estimate of 341,500.

The third row is the point. The same estimator that says the degree-57 graph should die at 20 branches — and that puts the expected number of complete structures at 10−412088 — says Hoffman–Singleton should die at 5, and Hoffman–Singleton exists. The constraints are positively correlated and the method is falsely pessimistic. A search stalling near the crossover is not evidence of non-existence: at degree 7 that identical signal would have been wrong.

The cost of brute force

Measured at degree 7: 81,381,110 search nodes, 61 seconds, to the first Hoffman–Singleton. Projecting the same tree-size model upward:

Lloyd's bound on the total computation available in the observable universe is about 10120 operations. The general search exceeds that by some 5947 orders of magnitude. Even the cyclic-restricted search — the dead-end one — exceeds it by 65.

Symmetry does not rescue it. The most any automorphism assumption can buy is the order of the assumed group: odd, at most 375, a factor of 102.6. And the assumption is not safe, since a trivial automorphism group is consistent with everything known, and Higman already ruled out the symmetric case that would have helped most.

Second pass: dropping the assumption, and a new constraint

Everything above measures a search everyone agrees is restricted. The obvious next question is what happens without the restriction.

The general search is tractable after all

Adding one branch to an existing structure turns every constraint into a binary disequality between two cells of the unknowns: with pi := σi,r, the triangle condition is just pi[σhi(x)] ≠ ph[x], because σhi is already known and the index is a constant. So adding a branch is a list-colouring problem, with no group assumption anywhere. From scratch it reaches 12 branches in about a minute.

The involution property

In both Moore graphs that exist, every triangle composite τ(a,j,b) = σba·σjb·σaj is not merely a derangement but a fixed-point-free involution — all 6 in Petersen, all 210 in Hoffman–Singleton, every one of cycle type (2,2,2). Equivalently every branch-pair Latin square is symmetric; equivalently each pair of branches sees the other 55 as a 1-factorization of K56.

It is not implied by the conditions every search enforces:

structureinvolutive composites
Petersen6 / 6
Hoffman–Singleton210 / 210
cyclic frontier, t = 1966 / 5814
general growth, t = 120 / 1320

What makes it credible rather than a coincidence is that it independently implies the published cyclic-impossibility theorem. In the cyclic model τ is a translation, and a translation is an involution only if it is by exactly 28 — which forces every triangle sum to 28, and then the quadrilateral (0,i,j,k) sums to 56 ≡ 0, violating its condition. Involutions therefore kill the cyclic ansatz at four branches. That is the Axioms 2026 result, recovered as a corollary of a property found from Hoffman–Singleton.

If the property holds at degree 57, every published t-subgraph is off-path. The 20-branch structures have 66 involutive composites out of 5814; no amount of compute extends them to the graph. That is sharper than “the ansatz is a dead end”.

Status: verified at degrees 3 and 7, conjectural at 57. A control experiment below gives quantitative evidence that it probably does not hold at 57 — so read this conditional as unlikely to fire.

The caveat that cuts against it, stated plainly because it matters: the whole evidence base is two graphs that are vertex-transitive and rank 3, with automorphism groups of order 120 and 252,000. The degree-57 graph is known not to be vertex-transitive, with |Aut| ≤ 375 of odd order. A property holding in the two most symmetric cases imaginable is exactly the kind that can be an artefact of symmetry rather than a theorem. I tried and failed to prove it from the local axioms; the natural attempts reach a 6-cycle and stop. What holds the other way is that it implies a theorem about degree 57 specifically, proved without any symmetry assumption — consistency, not proof.

Why no group ansatz can work

Hoffman–Singleton's bijections generate the full symmetric group S6, across eight distinct cycle types. Nothing about the individual σij is structured; what is rigid is the composites. Two ansätze tested to destruction:

ansatzcomposite isdegree 3degree 7
cyclic (published), x + aija translationfinds Petersenstalls at 5 of 7
reflection, gij − xalways an involutionfinds Peterseninfeasible

The reflection ansatz is worth noting because it gets the involution property for free, collapses every triangle condition to a parity condition solved by taking each gij odd, and leaves only quadrilaterals — a search over 28 values per pair rather than 56!. It builds the Petersen graph and dies at degree 7, and since Z6 is the only abelian group of order 6, no abelian reflection ansatz reaches Hoffman–Singleton either.

A validated constrained search, and Lean

Imposing the involution property, with backtracking, recovers Hoffman–Singleton (210/210 involutive), which validates the model. At degree 57 it stalls at four branches: the constraint is strong enough that blind search struggles, and the natural next step is to seed from an explicit 1-factorization of K56 rather than search blind.

A Lean file formalizes the block decomposition — the Moore condition in “friendship at distance two” form, block sizes, independence of blocks, and the matching lemma the whole reduction rests on. It is not machine-checked: this machine has no Lean toolchain and no network route to one.

Third pass: the gauged model

Fixing the gauge σ0j = id collapses the involution property into something far more usable. The triple {0,i,j} gives τ = σij outright, so every σij is itself a perfect matching on the 56 points (verified 1/1 for Petersen, 15/15 for Hoffman–Singleton). The quadrilateral through the root then forces matchings sharing an index to be edge-disjoint, so each branch's 55 matchings form a 1-factorization of K56 — exactly, since 55 × 28 = 1540 = |E(K56)|.

This is by far the best model found here:

At degree 57 it still returns UNKNOWN at eight branches — the involution property is that strong a constraint. So the next move was to stop searching and construct.

The obvious construction fails, structurally

The natural candidate indexes both branches and points by Z55 ∪ {∞} and uses the standard round-robin 1-factorization on both sides. Everything through the root holds by construction; everything else fails — 0 of 240 triangles fixed-point-free, unrepaired by any twist. The reason is clean: round-robin factors act as reflections a ↦ 2s − a, so a triangle composite is a ↦ 2(s₁−s₂+s₃) − a, whose fixed point exists because 2 is invertible mod 55 — 55 is odd. Any 1-factorization built from reflections on an odd-order group is disqualified outright.

Why the degree-7 construction cannot generalize

Hoffman–Singleton's gauged array has an exceptional property: its 15 branch-pairs map bijectively onto all 15 perfect matchings of K6 — the Sylvester duads-and-synthemes configuration behind the outer automorphism of S6. The coincidence that permits it is C(m,2) = (m−1)!!:

It holds at exactly m = 2 and m = 6 — precisely the two degrees where a Moore graph exists.

This is easy to over-claim, so read it carefully. It is not evidence of non-existence: more available matchings is more freedom, not less, and naive counting would favour existence at 56. What it explains is why no construction generalizes. At degrees 3 and 7 an exceptional coincidence supplies the entire algebraic scaffold; at 57 there is no scaffold, and 1,540 matchings must be chosen from 8.7 ×1036 with nothing forcing the choice. If the graph exists, it is probably not an algebraic object — which is consistent with its being non-vertex-transitive with |Aut| ≤ 375 of odd order.

A proof, not a search

The gauged model turns the reflection family into a counting question, and the counting settles it outright. Realise the non-root matchings as reflections Mij(x) = gij − x in a fixed abelian group G of order m. Then Mij is always an involution, it is fixed-point-free iff gij ∉ 2G, and Mij, Mil are edge-disjoint iff gij ≠ gil. So each branch needs m − 1 distinct labels in G \ 2G, and since |2G| = |G|/|G[2]|,

|G \ 2G| = m − m/|G[2]| ≥ m − 1  ⇔  G[2] = G  ⇔  m is a power of two.

Theorem. A reflection construction over an abelian group exists only when m = k−1 is a power of two. So it is impossible at degree 7 and at degree 57 — no search required.

The counting verdict is exactly “m is a power of two” in every case, and it cross-checks against the solver in both decidable ones: a Moore graph at degree 3, INFEASIBLE at degree 7.

The useful generalization: any ansatz must supply, per branch, 55 pairwise-disjoint fixed-point-free involutions — a full 1-factorization of K56. Translations by involutions in an abelian group of order 56 supply at most 7; reflections at most 49. That one criterion disposes of the natural algebraic families with no search at all, and it is the rigorous core of why constructions never generalize past degree 7.

What the property really says: a dihedral relation

Writing A = Mij, B = Mjl, C = Mli — three fixed-point-free involutions — the triangle condition (C·B·A)² = id is equivalent to C·B·A = A·B·C, and multiplying on the left by A gives A (C·B) A = (C·B)−1.

Mij conjugates Mli·Mjl to its inverse — the group generated by that permutation and Mij is dihedral, with Mij a reflection. Verified on 120/120 ordered triples of Hoffman–Singleton.

This also rules out the cheap counting obstruction before anyone hunts for one. Mjl and Mli share an index, so they are edge-disjoint; the union of two disjoint perfect matchings is a union of even cycles, so the cycles of Mli·Mjl come in equal-length pairs (verified 120/120), and an inverting fixed-point-free involution then always exists. No single triple is ever unsatisfiable. Any obstruction at degree 57 has to be global across all C(56,3) = 27,720 triples at once — precisely the regime solvers handle worst.

Testing my own conjecture, and finding against it

Every positive data point for the involution property comes from m = 2 and m = 6, which are small enough that things can be true by accident. The control question: for three pairwise edge-disjoint perfect matchings — which is what Mij, Mjl, Mli must be — how often is the composite an involution by chance?

This cuts against my own conjecture, and I am reporting it as such. At degree 7 the property is short by 2 orders of magnitude and the graph exists, so correlation bridges it easily. At degree 57 it is short by 914,871. The first-moment method is unreliable — shown earlier to be wrong at degree 7 by construction — but being wrong by 2 orders and by 900,000 are not the same kind of claim.

The likeliest reading is that a degree-57 Moore graph would not have the involution property. If so, the gauged model is searching for something that does not exist (a better explanation of its four-branch ceiling than solver weakness), and the earlier “off-path” claim about the published frontier was conditional on the conjecture and should be withdrawn to that extent. The cyclic ansatz stays dead on the independent grounds proved above — no order-56 automorphism, and the reflection bound.

Branch extension is a colouring problem — with no local obstruction

Adding a branch is exactly a colouring problem. The unknowns are the (t−1) × 56 cells pi[x] = σi,t(x); each row must be a permutation, so each row is a clique of size 56 and each colour class is a transversal — one cell per row. For the verified 19-branch structure the conflict graph is 1008 cells, 361-regular, and extending it means splitting those cells into 56 independent transversals of size 18.

That gives several cheap necessary conditions, and it is worth knowing that every one of them passes:

testresultsettles it?
an independent transversal existsyes, witness verifiedno
clique number vs. 56 valuesexactly 56no — tight
Hoffman chromatic boundχ ≥ 19.77no
ratio bound on independent setsα ≤ 51, need 18no
2 or 4 disjoint transversalsUNKNOWN at 300 sno

So the 19-branch structure has no local obstruction to being extended — it is not a dead end cheap mathematics can expose. The clique bound coming out at exactly 56 is the striking part: the colouring is a perfect fit, every row forced to use every value once, all cross-row conflicts absorbed without ever forcing a 57th colour.

That is the same verdict the dihedral analysis gave from the algebraic side. No single triple and no local test is ever unsatisfiable, so any obstruction at degree 57 has to be global — exactly the kind a solver cannot find and a counting argument cannot see.

Local search moves the assumption-free frontier to 14

Branch extension is a dense equitable-colouring problem, and CP-SAT is a poor fit for those — local search is the standard tool, and it had not been tried. The annealer keeps one permutation per row (so the row structure is maintained by construction rather than propagated) and moves by swapping two values within a row, biased towards cells in conflict. It runs at about 56,000 moves per second.

Validated first: it extends Hoffman–Singleton's 6-branch structure to 7 in 9,086 moves, and the result rebuilds as a genuine Moore graph of degree 7.

Handing the near-solution to CP-SAT as a hint, and separately running large-neighbourhood repair with the conflict-free rows frozen, both return UNKNOWN at 300 s. So 14 branches is the honest, assumption-free frontier reached here — up from 12.

The cyclic structures are hostile to extension

The same annealer on the verified 19-branch cyclic structure behaves completely differently:

structurebest cost after annealingrelative residual
general, 14 branches7 of 56,7840.012%
cyclic, 19 branches581 of 154,224 (44.3M moves)0.377%

General structures are driven to exactly zero, repeatedly. The cyclic structure cannot be moved below 581 violations by 44 million moves — a thirty-fold difference in relative residual, and a qualitative one: one kind completes, the other never approaches completion.

This matters because it is independent of the involution conjecture that the control experiment undermined. That earlier “off-path” claim was withdrawn; this is separate evidence for the same conclusion, resting only on measurement. The cyclic structures are not merely restricted — they are empirically hostile to being extended at all, even once the cyclic assumption is dropped and arbitrary permutations allowed.

Evidence, not proof: local search failing is not impossibility, and the two instances differ in size.

Verification: a checker, so a candidate would not rest on a script

Moore57Verify.lean defines an executable MooreCheck n k adj : Bool on an adjacency table together with MooreCheck_sound — the statement that its returning true implies the graph really is a Moore graph. Verifying a candidate then reduces to evaluating one Boolean on concrete data: decide for small cases, native_decide for n = 3250. That final theorem is the statement which would settle the problem; there is no candidate to plug in, but the last step would be machine-checked rather than trusted.

The Lean file is not machine-checked here (no toolchain, egress blocked), so checker.py mirrors it clause for clause and is tested: it accepts Petersen and Hoffman–Singleton, rejects a wrong degree claim, rejects all 266 one-edge perturbations of both, and rejects the circulant C10(1,5), which has the right order and degree but 4-cycles.

Now the accounting, and the first number is exact. With the gauge, a 3-branch structure is precisely a derangement of the 56 points, so the count on just 3 of the 57 branches is N3 = D56 ≈ 1074.4. Isomorph rejection helps at 3 branches — the classes are just cycle types — then stops helping. At 4 branches the count is ~D563 = 10223 against residual symmetry of at most 56! = 1074.9, so the isomorphism classes number at least 10148.

A guaranteed brute force in local compute time does not exist for this problem. The obstacle is not the implementation. Four branches out of fifty-seven already exceed any physically available budget by 135 orders of magnitude — computed from an exact count, with isomorph rejection already credited. The program is correct and will find the graph if one exists. It will not finish.

Conclusion

There is no point at which this becomes brute-forceable. The binding constraints buy a couple of orders of magnitude against a shortfall of thousands, and the shortfall is not an artefact of a bad formulation — the reduction used here is exact and is the one the published searches use. Two things follow that are worth acting on:

  1. The t = 20 frontier should not be read as evidence against existence. It is the ceiling of an ansatz that misses Hoffman–Singleton, and it sits exactly where a first-moment estimate that is demonstrably wrong at degree 7 says it should sit.
  2. The leverage is structural, not computational. The involutions result is the right shape: a trace–rank identity that closes a case without searching. The other tractable-looking target is Dalfó's tight-coclique residual — a distance-regular graph on 2850 vertices of degree 49 and diameter 3 — a strictly smaller object than the graph itself.
  3. Prove or refute the involution property at degree 57. It holds at every degree where a Moore graph exists, it is not implied by the conditions every published search enforces, and it independently re-derives a 2026 theorem. If it is a theorem, the published frontier is off-path and should be replaced by a 1-factorization-seeded search; if it is false at 57, that is itself a sharp structural asymmetry between degree 57 and the degrees that work.

What I did not get: the graph. Nothing here suggests it is within reach. The frontier moved from “20 branches under an ansatz that cannot work” to “12 branches under an honest model, 19 verified, plus a constraint saying the 20-branch structures were never on a path to a solution”. That is progress on the design, not on the construction.
