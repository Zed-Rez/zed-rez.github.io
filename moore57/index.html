The missing Moore graph

Where the constraints bind, and how far short of brute force that leaves us.

A Moore graph of degree 57 and diameter 2 would be a strongly regular graph with parameters (3250, 57, 0, 1): 57-regular, girth 5, 92,625 edges, 58,094,400 pentagons, spectrum 571 71729 (−8)1520. Degrees 2, 3 and 7 give the pentagon, the Petersen graph and the Hoffman–Singleton graph. Degree 57 is the only case left, open since 1960.

The question I set out to answer was practical: do the known results constrain the graph tightly enough that the remainder can be handed to a brute-force search? They do not, and the margin is not close. What follows is the audit, with everything checked in code.

The reachable frontier is 20 of the 57 branches the graph needs. The pruned search tree for the honest search is around 106067 nodes against a universe-wide budget of about 10120. The strongest structural constraint known — |Aut| ≤ 375, now of odd order — is worth 102.6.

Nothing classical rejects it

Integral spectrum, both Krein conditions, the absolute bound, a ratio bound of exactly 400, a clique bound consistent with triangle-freeness. Every standard test passes, which is why the problem survives: it has to be settled by construction, or by an argument specific to this parameter set. Each formula is validated against Petersen and Hoffman–Singleton first, including a brute-force pentagon count confirming the closed form n·C(k,2)·(k−1)/5.

The reduction

Root the graph. It has 57 neighbours, each with 56 further children: 57 blocks of 56. Girth 5 forces the edges between any two blocks to be a perfect matching σij, and is equivalent to the condition that every product around a 3-cycle or 4-cycle of blocks is a derangement. So the graph is exactly an edge-labelling of K57 by 1596 permutations of 56 points (1540 after gauge-fixing), subject to 29,260 triangle and 1,185,030 quadrilateral constraints. A structure on t < 57 blocks is a t-subgraph.

The reduction is implemented in both directions and checked end to end: Petersen and Hoffman–Singleton decompose into σ-tables, the tables satisfy the conditions, the tables rebuild the original graphs, and perturbing a single bijection breaks both.

The frontier, reproduced

Growing a labelling one branch at a time with CP-SAT reaches t = 19 of 57 and stalls there across every restart at 90 seconds per branch; the published frontier is 20. The certificate is verified twice — arithmetically, and by building the graph it describes: 1084 vertices, girth 5, with 19 vertices already at their full degree 57 and 1064 vertices at 19 of 57.

The ansatz that gets you there cannot find Hoffman–Singleton

Requiring every σij to lie in one regular cyclic group collapses the problem to arithmetic in Z56, which is what makes t = 20 reachable at all. But it is not a free assumption: if all the bijections lie in a common regular abelian group, then acting by a generator on every block is an automorphism of order k−1 fixing the root and all k of its neighbours. Searching that model exhaustively where the answer is known:

The group theory agrees, and can be read straight off the σ-table: an automorphism fixing the root and every layer-1 vertex acts on block i by some πi with πj σij = σij πi, so the whole group is computable by inspection.

At degree 7 the ansatz has nothing to find, which is why the exhaustive search stalls two branches short. At degree 57 the assumed automorphism has order 56 — even — which the no-involutions theorem forbids outright.

So t = 20 is not the frontier of the problem. It is the ceiling of a method that provably cannot reach 57, and that would have missed the last Moore graph anyone actually found.

Where the structure runs out — and why that proves nothing

Counting the expected number of ways to add each branch gives a crossover point, and that crossover tracks the empirical frontier closely:

Exact counts confirm the estimator's accuracy early on: 265 valid 3-block structures at degree 7, which the estimate gives exactly, and 101,040 valid 4-block structures against an estimate of 341,500.

The third row is the point. The same estimator that says the degree-57 graph should die at 20 branches — and that puts the expected number of complete structures at 10−412088 — says Hoffman–Singleton should die at 5, and Hoffman–Singleton exists. The constraints are positively correlated and the method is falsely pessimistic. A search stalling near the crossover is not evidence of non-existence: at degree 7 that identical signal would have been wrong.

The cost of brute force

Measured at degree 7: 81,381,110 search nodes, 61 seconds, to the first Hoffman–Singleton. Projecting the same tree-size model upward:

Lloyd's bound on the total computation available in the observable universe is about 10120 operations. The general search exceeds that by some 5947 orders of magnitude. Even the cyclic-restricted search — the dead-end one — exceeds it by 65.

Symmetry does not rescue it. The most any automorphism assumption can buy is the order of the assumed group: odd, at most 375, a factor of 102.6. And the assumption is not safe, since a trivial automorphism group is consistent with everything known, and Higman already ruled out the symmetric case that would have helped most.

Conclusion

There is no point at which this becomes brute-forceable. The binding constraints buy a couple of orders of magnitude against a shortfall of thousands, and the shortfall is not an artefact of a bad formulation — the reduction used here is exact and is the one the published searches use. Two things follow that are worth acting on:

  1. The t = 20 frontier should not be read as evidence against existence. It is the ceiling of an ansatz that misses Hoffman–Singleton, and it sits exactly where a first-moment estimate that is demonstrably wrong at degree 7 says it should sit.
  2. The leverage is structural, not computational. The involutions result is the right shape: a trace–rank identity that closes a case without searching. The other tractable-looking target is Dalfó's tight-coclique residual — a distance-regular graph on 2850 vertices of degree 49 and diameter 3 — a strictly smaller object than the graph itself.
