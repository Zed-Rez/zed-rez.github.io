How accurate is the die-finding?

Short answer: the total number of dies is about right, but the largest dies are wrong. When two photos show the same physical coin, the pipeline links them 96% of the time on the obverse and 99% on the reverse. But chains of weak matches have merged distinct obverse dies into a few impossible "dies" of hundreds of coins. About 15–20% of the obverse faces we put on a shared die are probably there by mistake. Separately, some real links are missed because the two coins were never compared. The two errors roughly cancel in the total, which is likely within about 2% of right.

What a die link is

Roman coins were struck by hand between two engraved dies: the obverse (head) die and the reverse (tail) die. Each die was cut separately, so every die has its own tiny differences in letter spacing, hair and drapery, even when it copies the same design. Two coins that share these differences were struck from the same die. A die study collects those links. They show how many dies an issue used, which reverse dies were used with which obverse dies, and the order the issue was struck in.

How the dies were found

The starting point is 421,249 photographed coin faces from museum, university and auction collections, each already catalogued to RIC (imperial) or RPC (provincial). No one labelled the dies. The pipeline works in six steps.

  1. Find the coin in the photo. A disc finder searches for the circle with the sharpest edge, then crops and rescales so every coin face is the same size and centred. Photos that show both sides in one frame are split into obverse and reverse.
  2. Decide which coins could share a die. Comparing every coin with every other is impossible, so the catalogue narrows the field. A reverse die strikes only one design, so reverses are compared within a single RIC or RPC type. Obverses are compared within the same ruler, denomination, obverse legend and bust type, with dates no more than 25 years apart. Faces with no possible partner, such as a type known from one coin, are compared instead with the 20 coins whose catalogue text is most similar (same ruler, mint and denomination). That gives about 15 million pairs.
  3. Line the two coins up. Two photos of coins from one die are never taken at the same angle, size or lighting. A quick low-resolution pass ranks each coin's candidates. The best are then aligned several ways (by rotation correlation, by matched image features, and by overlap), and the best alignment is kept.
  4. The point test. About 1,000–2,000 small textured points are chosen on one coin and each is searched for on the other. Half the points fix the alignment, and the score is the share of the other half that land within 4 pixels of where they should. Coins from one die agree point by point, so the score is high. Coins from two dies of the same design agree in the broad shapes but not in the fine detail, so the score is low. Most pairs score about 0.05, and true matches mostly score well above 0.25. No neural network judges die identity: an image model was tried first, and it mostly recognised which collection a photo came from.
  5. Join matches into dies. Every pair scoring above 0.25 is a match, and matches are joined strongest first: if A matches B and B matches C, all three go on one die. Merging two dies that both already have several coins needs a score of at least 0.25 (obverse) or 0.30 (reverse). The chaining recovers links the direct test missed, but it can also join two dies through one bad match. That is the source of the false merges described below.
  6. Connect the two sides. Each coin joins its obverse die to its reverse die. Those joins are the lines on this map, and a line is thicker when more coins share the pair. Pairs that came close but scored under the bar (0.15–0.25) are kept as "possible same-die" links.

How the rules were set. The thresholds were fixed in advance on one Roman Republican issue, Crawford RRC 361. Schaefer's hand-made die list covers every coin of it. They were then tested blind on a second issue, RRC 393/1a. There the pipeline put coins on the right die with 97% precision and 93% recall on the obverse, and 94% and 85% on the reverse. On that test, every coin was compared with every other. On the imperial corpus they are not, and that difference is the main finding below.

The result is 381,413 dies. Of these, 22,745 have two or more coins, and 62,581 faces sit on a shared die. The map shows the dies that link into chains. The tallest chains on the map are the false merges described below, so treat them with suspicion.

How we checked it against published die studies

A die study by hand records how many coins it saw (n) and how many obverse dies they came from (d). From these, Esty's estimator gives the number of dies the whole issue probably used, including dies that no surviving coin shows: D̂ = nd / (n − d).

That lets us ask a fair question of our own data. With m of our coins from the issue, how many should share a die with at least one other? If dies were equally common, the answer is 1 − (1 − 1/D̂)m−1. More coins, or fewer dies, make sharing more likely. We compare that expected share with the share our pipeline actually found. If we find much less sharing than expected, the pipeline is missing links. If we found much more, it would be merging dies that should be separate.

For each issue we take the published study's coins n and obverse dies d. From these, Esty's estimator gives the issue's total obverse dies, D̂ = nd / (n − d). With m of our own coins of that issue, the share expected to share an obverse die with at least one other is 1 − (1 − 1/D̂)m−1. Applied to each study's own sample, this formula lands within a few points of its published coverage.

The Nero denarii are the cleanest test: a large, well-photographed issue with a recent machine-assisted die study. Here we find somewhat less obverse die-sharing than expected and most of the reverse. Small gold issues, such as Postumus, come out close. Tetricus I and Laelianus come out badly. Pertinax has no die study, but 3% obverse sharing would imply about 1,800 obverse dies for an 87-day reign, which is not credible.

A caution about this comparison. Published die studies choose issues that interlink and collect near-complete corpora. Their raw coverage (median 0.92 across 87 Roman studies in the SILVER database) therefore says nothing about a scattered sample like ours, and it is not used here. Scaling each study's die count down to our sample size corrects for sample size. But issues chosen for study may have fewer dies than average, which would make our shortfall look bigger than it is. We estimate this pushes the expected shares 10–20% too high. For Nero, that lowers the obverse expectation from 0.41 to about 0.33–0.37, against our 0.31. That gap is about the size of the noise. Read the gaps above as upper bounds.

Checks that need no literature

These tests use only our own data, so the selection problem above does not touch them. They now carry the verdict.

  • Same coin, photographed twice (recall). The test set is 353 coins that appear twice in the corpus, re-photographed or re-sold, and confirmed as one coin by weight and an independent image check. A die-finder must link these. It linked 96% on the obverse and 99% on the reverse. Of the 17 misses, 15 were never compared at all. Only 2 were compared and scored too low.
  • Big obverse dies fall apart (precision). Inside a real die, most pairs of coins should match each other directly. Dies of 3 coins pass, with a median of 100% of pairs matching directly. Dies of 11–30 coins have a median of only 36%, and dies of more than 30 coins only 20%. The six largest obverse "dies" have 143–604 coins each, such as a Gordian III antoninianus die of 604 coins across 43 types. They are not joined by one bad match: hundreds of borderline matches (scores 0.27–0.31) each add one more coin.
  • Borderline matches are mostly wrong. We checked whether a match on one side is backed by a match on the other side of the same coins. Obverse matches scoring 0.25–0.30 are backed at only an eighth of the rate of clean ones. On the reverse, about half of matches in that band are false, and about 15% of all reverse matches. Lowering the threshold would add almost nothing true: of about 11,000 extra obverse links, fewer than 100 are backed.
  • Some pairs are never compared (recall). Of coin pairs with a confident reverse match, only 54% are also linked on the obverse, and about a quarter of those were never compared on the obverse. The usual reasons are very large comparison groups, coins missing a date or denomination, and the catch-all pools for coins with no obvious partner.
  • Where errors cluster. Third-century base-metal coins (antoniniani, Gordian III bronze), small museum photos, the largest comparison groups, and matches scoring under 0.35. Gold and high-resolution auction photos are the cleanest.
  • Some "impossible" matches are catalogue errors. For example, one coin may give a ruler's alternate legend, or a mint differ between two catalogues for what is one die. So simply forbidding matches across catalogue differences would throw away real links.

What changes next

  • Stricter merging in big groups. Raise the bar as a comparison group grows (0.25 up to 20 coins, 0.30 up to 100, 0.35 up to 300, 0.40 beyond), and need two supporting matches before joining two existing dies. Simulated on the existing scores, this cuts the largest obverse die from 604 coins to 80, removes about 8,400 mostly false obverse links, and nearly doubles the obverse cross-check rate. It loses none of the same-coin links. No new image matching is needed.
  • Compare the pairs the other side points to. Score the roughly 6,000 pairs that share a confident die on one side but were never compared on the other. That is under an hour of compute, for about 1,500 more obverse links.
  • Tidy the catalogue keys. Fill in missing dates and denominations so twins land in the same group, and count a coin listed in two catalogues once.
  • Calibrate against Schaefer. Score the Roman Republican dies, labelled coin by coin, to turn these relative checks into absolute precision and recall.
